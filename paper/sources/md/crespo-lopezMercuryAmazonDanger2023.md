Amazon conservation is essential for the global future. Mercury is currently among the worst global pollutants
and most (78.5%) of the South-American emissions are from the Amazon. Current Brazilian legislation on mining
activities and trade of gold, and economic interests in soy, beef and large-scale projects such as dams, are key
influences in mercury mobilization and emissions in the Amazon with the potential to affect the global environment. However, banning mercury in mining, while desirable, is not an efficient strategy if no other action is
taken. The interconnected issues, such as exports (soy, beef and gold) and energy generation, must be addressed
together to provide effective protection for human health and the environment. Realistically, to improve mercury
emissions in the Amazon, we must stop looking solely at “the single story” (a limited view of reality) of supposedly “artisanal and small-scale gold mining” in the region and understand the complex economic, social,
political, and international aspects of this problem. We propose some recommendations for international
agencies, governments, communities and the private sector.

1. Mercury as a global concern
Pollution currently causes more than 9 million deaths each year
globally, i.e. it is responsible for one in six deaths worldwide (Fuller
et al., 2022). This problem disproportionately affects low- and
middle-income countries, which account for more than 90% of these
pollution-related deaths (Fuller et al., 2022). Toxic chemical pollution
and ambient air pollution have been identified as main causes of the
increase in deaths (Fuller et al., 2022). Among the top chemicals of
major concern regarding public health and the environment, mercury is
ranked third due to its ubiquitous presence and deleterious consequences (ATSDR, 2022). Mercury is a liquid metal at room temperature
with many uses for human day-life due to its characteristics, such as
binding to other metals, electrical conductivity, and high surface tension. From production of electrical components (batteries, electrical
switches) and industrial compounds (chlorine gas, caustic soda) to
odontology and mining of precious metals (gold, silver), it is essential for
modern life; however, there is a high cost: thousands of tons of toxic
waste emitted and released to the environment per year. Since this metal
can travel long distances by air and ocean pathways (reaching regions as
far as the Arctic1), mercury contamination is a global challenge. In fact,
according to the United Nations (UN), it is related to 11 of the 17 UN
sustainable development goals (Fig. 1).
Globally, artisanal and small-scale gold mining (ASGM) is the
anthropogenic activity that contributes the most to mercury emissions to
air (UNEP, 2019). It also accounts for approximately two-thirds of all
anthropogenic mercury presently found in the oceans (UNEP, 2019).
Indonesia, Peru, and Brazil are currently the countries in the world that

emit most mercury from ASGM (Fig. 1). Worryingly, 78.5% of all South
America´s emissions originate in the Amazon (Galvis, 2018). To know
what is happening in the Amazon and to prevent the global consequences of this contribution, it is essential to understand the real context
related to this problem in the region.
2. ASGM in the Amazon and the Brazilian law
The UN defines ASGM as “gold mining conducted by individual miners
or small enterprises with limited capital investment and production. It is
typically a de-centralised production system. [.] Some [governments] define
it by the magnitude of ore processed (e.g., less than 300 tonnes per day) and
some by the mining and processing methods used (e.g., manual and/or semimechanised techniques)” (UNEP, United Nations Environment Programme, 2017a). Because gold in the Amazon is mostly found as alluvial
deposits (fine particles in sediment), miners use liquid mercury to capture the small particles. Subsequently, the amalgams are heated to
separate the gold, and some mercury is emitted into the atmosphere
during this process. To avoid a high impact to the environment,
Colombia recently banned the use of mercury in ASGM and industrial
activities.2 However, to improve the problem of mercury emissions and
its presence in the Amazon, realistically, it is essential to avoid
oversimplification.
The Amazon is the largest rainforest in the world, with an area of
approximately 7 million km2, and extends over nine countries; all of
them have reported environmental and human exposure to mercury
(Galvis, 2018). However, most (70%) of the Amazon is in Brazil;
therefore, actions carried out in this country are the main influence on

In 2020, despite the global slowdown in economic activities due to the
COVID-19 pandemic, the estimated gold production in Brazil reached 92
tons (an increase of 41% since 2015); of this, 90% comprised
non-industrial mining (Rodrigues and Leitão, 2022). Most (94%) of the
non-industrial mining areas are in the Amazon.3 Non-industrial mining
employs an average gold:mercury ratio of 1:4 (Castilhos and Domingos,
2018); thus, in 2020 alone, more than 300 tons of mercury were used in
an area of approximately 100,000 ha. Globally, it has been estimated
that approximately 35% of the total release of mercury from ASGM is
directly emitted to the atmosphere while the remainder is released into
the hydrosphere (rivers, lakes, soils, tailings) (Telmer and Veiga, 2008),
although this proportion could vary according to many factors such as
the forest surrounding mining areas (Gerson et al., 2022). Consequently,
the term “small-scale” for the gold mining in the Amazon does not imply
a minor presence or low impact of this occupational activity. Furthermore, until 1989, in Brazil, the ASGM and the ASGM miners (called
garimpeiro in Portuguese) were defined according to the rudimentary
nature and the use of simplified techniques and instruments; however,
later legislation eliminated this unique, traditional, rudimentary character (Ministério Público Federal, 2020). Currently, Brazilian legislation
defines the garimpeiro as someone who extracts certain mineable substances (including gold), regardless of the extraction technique, and
immediately uses the deposit without prior assessment of the environmental impact; furthermore, the definition is limited to the mineral

typology and location (e.g., gold in a non-protected area of the Amazon).
Each mining license for individuals (not companies) allows exploration
of up to 10,000 ha with any type of machinery and without prior environmental impact assessment. Many news articles and reports, especially in recent years,4 have illustrated the frequent use of dredging rafts,
excavators, airplanes, and water cannons, among other heavy machinery, for ASGM in the Amazon (Fig. 2). The employment of this technology is surprising, considering that the Brazilian Amazon is the least
developed region in a developing country (Brazil). For example, an
excavator costs around 250–300 times the minimum wage, and a
dredging raft ranges from 80 to 800 times the minimum wage. Thus, at
least in the Brazilian Amazon, the terms “artisanal” and “small-scale” are
no longer accurate descriptions of the gold mining.
Once the gold has been extracted, Brazilian law requires that the gold
can only be sold to companies authorized by the Central Bank (called
Distributors of Bonds and Securities, or DTVMs in Portuguese) (Rodrigues and Leitão, 2022). DTVMs can then sell this gold to all the other
agents of the gold market such as jewelry stores and holding/trading
companies for export. DTVMs record the gold extraction location on a
registration form as per the garimpeiro´s declaration without any proof.
Therefore, the origin of gold in the Brazilian Amazon is controlled by a
“good faith” system. Furthermore, DTVMs are exempt, by law, from any
liability for the veracity of this information. The owners/CEOs of DTVMs
can obtain mining licenses, become partners in companies related to
each link in the gold chain (such as air transport companies or holdings),
or have family relationships with the owners/CEOs of these companies
(Rodrigues and Leitão, 2022). These facts, are closely related to the
increased environmental impact and violence observed in recent years,

This system allows gold from protected areas and/or high impact
chains to enter the international market. In 2021, 54% of Brazilian gold
exports went to Canada and Switzerland (approximately $2.83 billion).6
In that year, the state of São Paulo alone (a state more than 2000 km
away from the Amazon) exported 25.4% of all Brazilian gold exports
(approximately $1.35 billion); however, there is not a single gold mining
area in that state, which means that the environmental cost was for
another region (probably the Amazon, where the 94% of non-industrial
mining areas are located). At the same time, Amazonian populations
have the lowest Human Development Index in Brazil7; indeed, most
people that work directly with mercury are far from rich. This
complexity has led to questions like those recently asked by Alessandra
Korap Munduruku (of the Munduruku indigenous population), “who are
the gold buyers? […] who is selling mercury?”.8

Despite the clear discrepancy between the international concept of
ASGM and the reality of gold mining in the Brazilian Amazon, the term
ASGM continues to be used, particularly in international reports, when
referring to the mining activities responsible for mercury emissions in
this region. The case of the Brazilian Amazon clearly demonstrates that
governments can change the definition, based on their stance on mining.
This situation is potentiated by allowing countries to have their own
country-specific definitions, as proposed by the UN and the Minamata
Convention on Mercury (UNEP, United Nations Environment Programme, 2017b), but, at the same time, maintaining the same ASGM
term for international reports. Indeed, this discrepancy contributes to
the persistence of false impressions about the extent and impact of
mining-related environmental contamination in the Amazon. Instead,
international efforts should be devoted to finding a way to implement a
consistent definition of ASGM, as a first step out of the mirage of “the
single story” (as described by the writer, Chimamanda Adichie, when
referring to default assumptions and conclusions, based on a limited
view of reality9). When we discuss this problem from the context of a
single story, we are prevented from forming a more complex, nuanced
understanding, which could lead us to effective decisions.

mercury deposition would continue to increase, because of legacy
mercury (UNEP, 2019). Worryingly, the combination of legacy mercury
and climate change would increase the transformation of mercury into
the most toxic form, methylmercury, which can readily enter the food
chain to affect all living beings (UNEP, 2019). Recent evidence has
revealed that environmental mercury (i.e., in soil, litterfall, and leaves)
can accumulate faster in the intact rainforest around ASGM regions than
in areas within mining regions, because the rainforest acts as a “sink” for
mercury emissions (Gerson et al., 2022). Moreover, mercury that accumulates in the rainforest can potentially be emitted back into the atmosphere, in the event of a fire and/or deforestation. These facts are
critical, considering the historical records of biomass burning and
deforestation in the Amazon, during the past three years (Crespo-Lopez
et al., 2021). Following the extensive fires that occurred in 2019 and
2020, in 2022 we have witnessed the largest deforestation since the
records began in 2015.10 After removing the wood, the fields are typically burned to create pasture areas and to allow the illegal appropriation of protected territories. Interestingly, 62% of potentially illegal
deforestation has been promoted by the prospect of exporting soy and
beef to the European Union (Rajão et al., 2020). Thus, fires and deforestation have increased the dynamics of mercury in the Amazon, in
terms of environmental contamination and the intoxication of living
beings (including humans). Controlling the origin of Brazilian soy and
beef imported by the European Union could have a positive impact on
both deforestation and the mobilization of mercury in the Amazon.
Additionally, other economic interests can also mobilize legacy
mercury. The Amazon includes four of the largest hydroelectric power
plants (HPPs) in the world (Tucuruí, Belo Monte, Jirau and Santo
Antônio). Additionally, more than 334 dams are in operation or under

construction, and another 400 are in planning or awaiting approval
(Winemiller et al., 2016). Damming Amazonian rivers can potentially
favor the proliferation of bacteria responsible for transforming legacy
mercury into methylmercury that easily enters and magnifies up the
food chain (Crespo-Lopez et al., 2021), in addition to other impacts, such
as limiting the migration of Amazonian fish (e.g., Brachyplatystoma sp.)
which contributes to the accumulation of mercury in these species.
Recent evidence has demonstrated that populations living near HPPs
may have similar or even higher hair mercury levels than the levels
found in populations near mining regions (Arrifano et al., 2018a and
Arrifano et al., 2018b). These data support the notion that dams have the
potential to mobilize mercury, and consequently, contribute to environmental contamination and human exposure.
4. Concluding remarks and recommendations
Presently, the huge emissions from the Amazon are likely to influence environmental contamination and human exposure globally,
because mercury can travel long distances by air and ocean pathways.
However, banning mercury, while desirable, is not an efficient strategy,
if no other action is taken. The interconnected issues related to the
release and mobilization of mercury must be addressed together to
provide effective protection for human health and the environment
(Fig. 3). In this context, it would be highly recommendable that international agencies such as UNEP and the Minamata Convention on
Mercury discuss the implementation of a consistent definition of ASGM,
establishing the differences with what is happening in the Amazon. To
control mercury emissions in the Amazon, we must consider the complex economic, social, political, and international aspects of the
problem.
UNEP recommendations mainly focus on awareness strategies in
mining communities and the use of alternative mercury-free mining.
However, often the option of using mercury or not is not a free choice for
the people that work directly with the metal and their families. Violence,
inequality, hunger, and poverty are intimately linked to the inefficacy of
these strategies. To guarantee the implementation of these mercury-free

that make it possible to prohibit and penalize conflicts of interest in gold
extraction and trading companies/CEOs (Fig. 3). In the entire gold chain
(including international market and involved countries), we need a
system that does not contribute to the growth of exploitation and
inequality, but that protects the environment and people, and forces a
fairer distribution of wealth. In Brazil, it is especially important that the
significant dismantling carried out in recent years of control agencies,
such as the Instituto Brasileiro do Meio Ambiente e dos Recursos Naturais
Renováveis (IBAMA) and the Instituto Chico Mendes de Conservaç ão da
Biodiversidade (ICMBio), be urgently reversed. This action would ensure
that the Amazon can once again become a safe region to live in.
Recommendations on how avoid mobilization of legacy mercury
have seldom been provided. Considering the most recent evidence,
protecting the rainforest around mining areas, so that this forest remains
intact, is an essential measure to avoid increased emissions. Furthermore, we need international regulations to control the origins and trade
of Brazilian soy and beef, in addition to gold. At this time when Brazil
has changed its government to one that promises to be committed to the
protection of the Amazon, a unique opportunity opens for European and
North American countries make a difference by supporting (and
demanding) these regulations, in addition to Brazilian voices being
raised. For this, solutions such as tracking codes or verifiable designations of origin could be implemented, adding ecological value to the
“clean” products (Fig. 3). This ecological value could be added to “clean”
energy, especially those relating to the development of large-scale projects in the Amazon. Ecocides and ethnocides like those recently caused
by the HPP Belo Monte should not be repeated.11
The Amazon is the responsibility of all of us and the mercury challenge is intimately linked to its future, as well as our future. We can
change the current situation facing the complex and nuanced reality,
and all (companies, governments, communities, international agencies,
and non-governmental organizations) must contribute.

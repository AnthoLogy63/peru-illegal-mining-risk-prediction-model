Abstract
The Amazonian ecosystems of Madre de Dios (MDD) region, considered Peru’s “capital of biodiversity” due to its impressive
species richness, are threatened by the rapid expansion of infrastructure. The paving of the Interoceanic Highway in 2010 has
brought rapid population growth and environmental degradation, threatening large-scale conservation efforts such as the
Vilcabamba-Amboro Conservation Corridor (VACC). To quantify the impacts of development processes and to identify critical
areas where further conservation efforts should be focused, we analyzed 20 years of land use and land cover (LULC) dynamics,
drivers, and fragmentation patterns in MDD. We mapped LULC in 1993, 2003, and 2013 using Landsat imagery and modeled the
proximate drivers of change related to deforestation, forest regrowth, gold mining, agriculture, and urban expansion in two time
periods (1993–2003 and 2003–2013). We characterized habitat fragmentation patterns in four regional target areas. Our results
show accelerated deforestation triggered by the road network spreading, mining and agriculture expansion, and population growth.
LULC changes produced two contrasting spatial landscape patterns: numerous small forest fragments in the north due to agricultural expansion along the Interoceanic Highway and large continuous forest losses caused by gold mining expansion in the south.
Current unplanned development in this region is threatening its outstanding biodiversity. We suggest that further conservation
efforts should focus on strengthening the legal framework of the VACC, implementing a sustainable management plan for MDD,
promoting agroforestry strategies along the Interoceanic Highway, and preserving smaller corridors for animal movement.

Introduction
Tropical forests sustain extraordinary biodiversity and play a
fundamental role in ecological, biophysical, biogeochemical,
and climatic processes providing key ecosystem services that
benefit human well-being from local to global scales (Foley
et al. 2007; Laurance et al. 2009). Among tropical forests, the
western Amazon has remarkable conservation value due to its
extraordinary biological (Ceballos and Ehrlich 2006; Young
et al. 2005) and cultural diversity (Killen 2007) and its lower
levels of land conversion when compared with the eastern
Amazon (Finer et al. 2008; Killen 2007). Nevertheless, land
conversion, road network, and hydrocarbon exploitation projects are currently expanding and threatening the natural and
cultural resources of the region (Asner et al. 2013; Finer et al.
2008; Oliveira et al. 2007).
The Madre de Dios (MDD) region of Peru, located in the
western Amazon, is an area of particular concern because of
its high levels of species richness, landscape protection efforts,
and strategic location to preserve the rainforest biodiversity

(Huertas 2007). MDD is part of the Tropical Andes biodiversity hotspot and has been considered “the global epicenter of
biodiversity” (CEPF 2005, p5) and a priority ecoregion
(Southwestern Amazonian Moist Forest) for global conservation in WWF’s Global 200 (Olson and Dinerstein 2002). Since
the 1970s, the Peruvian government has created and recognized several protected areas (PAs) and private conservation
areas, covering almost 50% of its territory (SERNANP 2017).
Major PAs include the Manu National Park, the Amarakaeri
Communal Reserve, the Tambopata National Reserve, and the
Bahuaja-Sonene National Park, which form the central portion of the Vilcabamba-Amboro Conservation Corridor
(VACC; Fig. 1). This corridor was established in the late
1990s to preserve biodiversity, ecosystems, and lands of various ethnic groups across the Tropical Andes and the western
Amazon (Bennett and Mulongoy 2006; CEPF 2005). It connects 30 million hectares, covering 18 PAs, multiple-use areas,
and indigenous reserves from the Vilcabamba mountains in
Peru to the Amboró National Park in Bolivia (Bennett and
Mulongoy 2006; CEPF 2005). The VACC aims to safeguard
the continuity of ecosystem processes, functions, and services
and allows wildlife movements across national borders.
Nevertheless, protected and unprotected areas of the corridor
are threatened by unplanned development and resulting land
use and land cover (LULC) changes in MDD.

In the last decades, MDD experienced significant LULC
changes facilitated by a mix of public policies such as market
incentives, neoliberal austerity, and regional integration
through large infrastructure (Alvarez and Naughton-Treves
2003; Chavez 2014). A significant driver of change in the
region has been the Interoceanic Highway, a binational project
signed in 1979 (Dourojeanni 2006), paved throughout the
2000s, and finished in 2010. The Interoceanic Highway connects the Pacific ports of Peru with Brazil (Killen 2007) and has
induced immigration to MDD and the growth of secondary
roads (Asner et al. 2013; Killen 2007; Scullion et al. 2014).
Consequently, the region experienced rapid economic and population growth, switching from mainly forest-based extractive
industries (e.g., Brazil nut) to small-scale agriculture, cattle
ranching, small- and medium-scale gold mining, and urban
development (Alvarez and Naughton-Treves 2003; Delgado
2008; Dourojeanni 2006; Giudice et al. 2012). Unfortunately,
MDD has not yet adopted its land management plan and continues to develop without coordination, resulting in overlapping
land use concessions causing economic losses, social conflicts,
poverty, and ecosystem degradation (CEPF 2005; Chavez et al.
2013; Dourojeanni 2006; Scullion et al. 2014).
Current LULC changes in MDD are increasingly reducing
landscape connectivity in the VACC (Rosenthal et al. 2012).

(Rosenthal et al. 2012). Deforestation typically impacts landscape structure (Southworth et al. 2004) by reducing total
forest area and partitioning large forest extents into smaller
patches (Haddad et al. 2015). Different landscape fragmentation patterns can reduce animal population size, cause declines
in species richness and persistence, increase mortality risks,
and ultimately cause local extinctions (Fischer and
Lindenmayer 2007; Haddad et al. 2015; Rutledge 2003).
Although some studies have analyzed LULC changes and
landscape fragmentation in MDD at local scales (Alvarez and
Naughton-Treves 2003; Chavez et al. 2013; Chavez 2014;
Scullion et al. 2014), none have analyzed the changes of landscape structure through time nor assessed the proximate
drivers of change at the regional scale. To understand how
much, where, and how LULC change is affecting MDD’s
natural resources, we address the following questions: (i)
“How much LULC change has there been in MDD for the
1993–2003 and 2003–2013 periods?”, (ii) “What are the main
proximate drivers of these LULC changes?”, (iii) What are the
links between deforestation and habitat fragmentation patterns?”, and (iv) “What critical areas should conservation efforts focus on in MDD?”. To answer these questions, we
quantify crucial information about LULC change, identify
proximate drivers, and assess resulting fragmentation patterns
at the regional scale. Our goal is to contribute critical information for the development of informed policies that will enhance biodiversity and habitat conservation in MDD.

Methods
Study area
MDD is located in the southeastern Peruvian Amazon bordering Brazil and Bolivia (Fig. 1). This 85,000-km2 region holds
a population of around 135,000 inhabitants (average of 1.57
person per km2; INEI 2015). MDD is also home to some of
the last uncontacted indigenous groups in the world living in
voluntary isolation (Huertas 2007). The region’s average annual temperature is 26.4 °C, with average annual relative humidity of 84.2%. The MDD rainy season is typically between
November and April (INEI 2010), and the total annual precipitation between 1995 and 2015 was 2214 mm (INEI 2012).
MDD was designated Peru’s “capital of biodiversity” by law
(Peruvian Law N° 26,311). The region has two biophysical
units: the sub-Andean mountain range (500–3967 m) dominated by a premontane tropical moist forest landscape and the
Madre de Dios river valley (176–500 m) dominated by a soft
relief with floodplains and low hills (Mateo and Cornejo
2006). MDD contains several life zones from tropical and

Page 3 of 14

subtropical humid, very humid and rain forests to premontane,
montane, and subalpine paramos coupled with four biogeographic provinces: Tropical Amazon, Subtropical Amazon,
Subtropical Yungas, and Subtropical Puna (Mateo and
Cornejo 2006). MDD hosts extraordinary biodiversity: 6809
plant, 1212 bird, 272 freshwater fish, 256 mammal, 183 amphibian, and 143 reptile recorded species (GBIF 2018).
Despite the biological importance of MDD, land conversion
persists inside and outside PAs (Finer and Novoa 2015; Finer
et al. 2016a, b; Novoa et al. 2016). National economic growth,
overlapping land use concessions, and the rise of international
gold prices are important documented drivers of deforestation
in the region (Chavez et al. 2013; Southworth et al. 2011;
Swenson et al. 2011). Major infrastructure projects (e.g., highways, railroads, power and gas lines, hydrocarbon projects)
planned for the western Amazon (Finer and Novoa 2015;
Laurance et al. 2009) are expected to accelerate future
LULC changes impacting MDD and the VACC.

Land Use and Land Cover mapping
To quantify how much LULC change there has been in MDD,
we used Landsat multispectral satellite images from 1993,
2003, and 2013 dry seasons (July–September; Asner et al.
2013). We used Landsat 5 Thematic Mapper images for
1993 maps; Landsat 7 Enhanced Thematic Mapper (ETM)
images prior to May 31, 2003, (to avoid scan line errors) for
2003 map; and Landsat 8 Operational Land Imager for 2013
maps. We developed cloud cover masks of no data for the
pixel labeled as cloud or cloud shadow for each year using
the quality assessment (QA) bands. This process eliminated
most clouds in the scenes. We addressed the across track degradation of radiometric response (Hansen and Loveland 2012;
Potapov et al. 2012) by standardizing each Landsat scene and
each band using the Moderate-resolution Imaging
Spectroradiometer (MODIS) bidirectional distribution function (BRDF) MCD43A4 product (Graesser and Ramankutty
2017). We then mosaicked cloud-free adjacent images (pathrows 20–68, 20–69, 30–67, 30–68, 30–69, 40–67, 40–68, and
40–69) for each year.
From the reflectance values of each pixel of the three mosaics, we calculated six variables: Enhanced vegetation index
(EVI), Normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI),
Tasseled Cap brightness (Tass1), Tasseled Cap greenness
(Tass2), and Tasseled Cap wetness (Tass3), and Principal
components analysis (PCA). We generated the training and
validation data for each land cover class in each year
(12,914 pixels for the 3 years) using the Land Mapper
(2015) application developed by the University of Puerto
Rico (Alvarez-Berríos and Aide 2015). This application allows users to manually draw polygons around groups of
Landsat pixels of a given land cover type based on visual
interpretation of very high resolution (> 3-m resolution)

8

Page 4 of 14

Google Earth images overlaid over the landscape grid. We
focused only on the most prominent and unequivocal land
cover classes in the study area: bare soil, water, forest (trees),
grassland (natural herbaceous vegetation, agriculture, and pastures combined), wetland (forested and unforested wetlands or
aguajales), and gold mining (bare soils, tailings, and ponds
associated with mining). Herbaceous vegetation, agriculture,
and pastures were combined into a single class to ensure the
best results from the classification algorithm and could later be
sorted into useful classes during the manual editing process
(see below). Many very high resolution images were available
for the region in 2013, fewer in 2003, and none in 1993.
Therefore, training and validation data for 2003 and 1993
scenes were partially and entirely collected from visual interpretation of the Landsat mosaic, as it was done in other studies
(e.g., Potapov et al. 2017).
To create a classification model, we first associated each
training and validation pixel (which has been assigned a year
and a cover class) with the variable values for the pixel from
the same year. This data set was used as input in a random
forest (RF) tree-based classifier (Liaw and Wiener 2002). For
the RF analysis, we used 25 terminal nodes, 500 trees, and a
minimum of 5 terminal nodes per tree. A model was created
for each year (1993, 2003, and 2013) using 50% of the data,
the other 50% being used for validation. We assigned land
cover classes to pixels based on the most votes (probability
> 60%). The final RF model had an overall accuracy > 90% in
the three models.
To correct misclassifications of some land classes (e.g.,
bare soil versus gold mining) in the 30-m resolution maps,
we did extensive manual editing and applied a majority filter
(3 × 3 window) to reduce noise. Because we were assessing
the dynamics of natural (i.e., forest) and human (i.e., pastures,
mining) land uses, we modified LULC maps in three ways.
First, since natural sand banks in rivers and bare soil related to
slash and burn agriculture (extensively checked using high
resolution imagery and located along the Interoceanic
Highway) were both initially classified as bare soil, we considered all bare soil pixels within 300 m of navigable rivers as
“sand banks.” This distance was the average length of sand
banks on the main navigable rivers in MDD observed in very
high resolution imagery in Google Earth. Second, we merged
the remaining bare soil pixels (related to slash and burn agriculture) and grassland classes into a single class called
“Agriculture.” The “Agriculture” class contained patches of
natural grasslands such as the Pampas del Heath and some
high Andean grasslands that represent a limited surface area
and, based on the satellite imagery we collected, are stable
through time. Most if not all changes in this class therefore
show changes in agricultural lands (slash and burn and pasture). Third, we inserted urban areas manually by hand digitizing each settlement (n = 37) using very high resolution imagery in Google Earth (for 2003 and 2013) and the Landsat

mosaics (for 1993 and 2003). We selected only cities and
towns that were visually identifiable, and their extent covered
the main urban core area as well as other scattered sub-urban
land use. We performed an accuracy assessment of the final
maps following the FAO (2016), Rossiter (2004), and
Olofsson et al. (2014) methodology. We generated equally
sized random samples for each land cover class to ensure all
land cover classes were represented (Congalton and Green
2008). We visually assessed the random pixels’ LULC with
the corresponding very high resolution imagery and Landsat
mosaics. For the interpretation process, we discarded samples
located in cloud cover. AMSC double-checked all pixels containing a mixture of classes and assigned the pixel with the
majority class (> 60%). Following Congalton and Green
(2008), we evaluated between 77 and 80 samples per land
cover class (forest, bare soil, agriculture, gold mining, urban,
and water) resulting in 1432 stratified random pixels evaluated
from the 3 years. We calculated the producer and user accuracy estimates per class and the area of change per transition
with their respective 95% confidence intervals.

Biophysical and socioeconomic data
We incorporated 23 anthropogenic and biophysical variables
to determine their potential role as proximate drivers of LULC
change in MDD (Table 1). Land use variables included agricultural titles, land concessions, and protected areas.
Environmental variables included geology, elevation, slope,
temperature, and precipitation. Accessibility variables included distance to paved and unpaved roads, road density, navigable rivers, non-navigable rivers, and towns. The demographic
variable used was total population density. All inputs were
resampled (bilinear interpolation) to 90-m resolution.

Drivers of change
We focused on six main LULC transitions identified and related
to deforestation (forest to agriculture, mining, and urban), forest
regrowth (agriculture to forest), mining expansion (forest and
agriculture to mining), and urban expansion (forest and agriculture to urban). To identify the proximate drivers of change from
the six transitions in the two time periods (1993–2003 and
2003–2013), we used the weights of evidence method (WoE;
Bonham-Carter et al. 1989; Goodacre et al. 1993) implemented
in Dinamica EGO, an environmental modeling platform
(Soares-Filho et al. 2013; Soares-Filho et al. 2009). We chose
Dinamica EGO due to its high performance, flexibility, openaccess, and user-friendly interface (Ferreira et al. 2019). The
WoE method has been extensively described in the literature
on LULC change studies, and it has been used to characterize
and quantify LULC transitions as well as to identify the influence of multiple spatial features on the observed transitions
(Molin et al. 2017; Soares-Filho et al. 2014). Similar to the

method that measures the association of each potential explanatory variable to the spatial probability of a transition to create
multiple transition probability maps (in our case, n = 6 for the
two time periods; Soares-Filho et al. 2009). Based on Bayesian

probability, the method calculates the weight of each range of
values of each spatial variable by contrasting them with the
observed transitions in a pair of LULC maps, accounting for
the number and location of pixels that had a transition or stayed
in the same LULC class (Osis et al. 2019).

To reduce computational time in the modeling process and
to match the original resolution of relevant inputs, we
resampled (bilinear interpolation) all maps to 90-m resolution.
Prior to the WoE analysis, we categorized continuous variables into discrete ranges following Soares-Filho et al.
(2009). The method assigned WoE values (+/−) to every range
of values (or category) of each variable that might influence
each transition. Positive weight values (WoE+) are attributed
to pixels where the transition under analysis occurred and
negative weight values (WoE−) to pixels where the transition
did not occur. We verified variable independence and the degree of association for all variables in all transitions by
performing a spatial correlation analysis using Cramer’s coefficient (V) based on chi-square for both periods (Almeida et al.
2003). V ranges between 0 and 1, where values closer to 1
show a stronger relationship between two variables.
Therefore, we removed auto-correlated variables that had V
values higher than 0.6 (Soares-Filho et al. 2009). As a result,
in the analysis of the first period (1993 to 2003), we removed
agricultural titles from five transitions (forest to agriculture,
mining, and urban and agriculture to urban and forest). In the
second period (2003 to 2013), we removed agricultural titles
from four transitions (forest to agriculture and urban and agriculture to urban and mining), protected areas from two transitions (forest to agriculture and mining), mining concessions
from one transition (forest to agriculture), and elevation from
one transition (forest to mining). In order to evaluate the total
strength and usefulness of all remaining variables, we used the
information value (IV; Siddiqi 2006). IV is an index developed from information theory (Kullback 1978), it is calculated
using the WoE coefficients (+/−) for each variable range, and
its absolute value is summed across ranges to obtain the final
IV value of the variable. IV values less than 0.02 indicate the
variable is not useful for modeling, 0.02–0.1 indicate a weak
relationship, 0.1–0.3 indicate a medium strength relationship,
and above 0.3 indicate strong relationship to the WoE odds
ratio (Siddiqi 2006).

Landscape fragmentation metrics
To estimate how deforestation led to different habitat fragmentation patterns and to identify critical areas for biodiversity
conservation, we calculated four landscape metrics in 1993,
2003, and 2013 LULC maps: (1) forest cover (FC); (2) number of patches (NP); (3) largest patch index (LPI; area of the
largest patch, expressed as a percentage of the total landscape
area); and (4) mean patch size (MPS). We performed these
analyses in four target areas (Fig. 1): Area 1 (MDD), the entire
region, to have a complete view of the fragmentation process;
Area 2 (Buffer), a 50-km buffer zone along the Interoceanic
Highway and all secondary roads where the greatest LULC
have occurred in the past 20 years (Chavez et al. 2013;
Delgado 2008); Area 3 (North), intersection between 50-km

buffer zone and the northern portion of the region (outside the
VACC) where non-forest areas (i.e., urban areas, roads, crops,
and pastures) doubled in size in only one year (2010–2011;
Chavez et al. 2013); and Area 4 (South), intersection between
50-km buffer zone and the VACC to the south where the
overall extent in gold mining has greatly expanded in the past
8 years (Asner et al. 2013; Scullion et al. 2014). We gave
special attention to the VACC since any change in land cover
and fragmentation in this corridor would have serious negative
implications for biodiversity, ecosystem services, and ecological function. This study provides a general description of
habitat at the landscape scale and identifies general fragmentation patterns in the areas most impacted by land change at
the local scale.

Results
Land use dynamics
The final six classes LULC maps had overall accuracies of
97.9% in 1993 (95% CI: 95.5–100%), 98.2% in 2003
(95.8–100%), and 97.9% in 2013 (95.6–100%;
Supplementary Material Table S1). The producer’s accuracies ranged from 27.6 (bare soil in 1993; 95% CI: 18.6–
36.5%) to 100% (urban in 2003), while the user’s accuracies ranged from 55.8 (water in 1993; 44.7–67.0%) to
98.8% (forest in 2013; 96.3–100%). Individual class accuracy for forest was usually high (99.4% and 98.7% average
producer’s and user’s accuracy) for the 3 years. In contrast,
bare soil accuracy was generally low (39.8% average producer’s accuracy) as well as water (68.9% average user’s
accuracy). Bare soil was confused with mining, which was
expected since alluvial mines are composed of mixed areas
of bare soil and standing water. In addition, water was
confused with the forest class mostly in backswamp forest
areas where aguaje palms (Mauritia flexuosa L.f.) and
standing water dominate. Nevertheless, these inter-class
confusions do not significantly affect the quantification
of the land transitions of interest in this study.
Forest was the predominant land cover class in the last
20 years (Fig. 2) covering 97.1% of MDD area
(8,232,803 ± 2558 ha) in 1993, 96.0% (8,139,795 ±
2509 ha) in 2003, and 95.2% (8,067,014 ± 2399 ha) in
2013. Agriculture and mining classes covered only 0.5%
(39,861 ± 2.4 ha) and less than 0.1% (3618 ± 0.2 ha) of
the region in 1993, respectively. The area of each of these
classes increased by 2.1% (180,266 ± 2503 ha) and 0.2%
(16,189 ± 0.8 ha) in 2003, respectively, and to 2.7%
(227,101 ± 2395 ha) and 0.4% (37,535 ± 1.3 ha) in 2013,
respectively. Urban, bare soil and water land cover classes
occupied collectively around 2% of the landscape and
changed slightly across time.

Land use between 1993 and 2003 was characterized by
greater deforestation (64,051 ± 30.8 ha) than forest regrowth
(11,454 ± 0.1 ha) with a net loss of forest cover of 52,597 ha
(Fig. 3a). Forest was mostly converted to agricultural areas,
while forest regrowth mainly occurred in abandoned croplands. Among all transitions, agricultural expansion had the
highest expansion (54,774 ± 21.1 ha) with a 352% increase.
Mining and urban expansion showed the lowest surface of
change (10,792 ± 8.7 and 576 ± 1.1 ha, respectively); however, mining had the second highest increase (348%). Mining

Discussion
Our results show that MDD lost over 168,000 ha (2%) of
primary tropical forests between 1993 and 2013. The forest
was converted mainly to agriculture, mining, and urban areas.
In this study, the main proximate drivers of change were road
network development, expansion of previous mining and agriculture patches, and population growth, which were likely
facilitated by public policies and institutional changes. As a

result, the region showed two contrasting spatial landscape

Landscape changes in the region
We found an increase in net deforestation rates from 6405 ha/
year from 1993 to 2003 to 10,466 ha/year from 2003 to 2013.
Our estimates for the second period concur with the national
forest and the global forest watch assessments showing a deforestation rate of 10,113 (2003–2013) and 10,789 ha/year
(2001–2014), respectively (Global Forest Watch 2014;
MINAM 2017). Between 2003 and 2013, the deforestation
rates detected in MDD were lower when compared with other
Peruvian Amazon regions (Loreto = 24,757 and Ucayali =
21,439 ha/year) where forest loss has been increasing since
the 1990s (Oliveira et al. 2007). Nevertheless, due to its smaller size, MDD’s lower deforestation rate has a greater relative
impact on the total deforestation proportion: 1.22%, while

Loreto and Ucayali’s were 0.67% and 2.09%, respectively.
Forest regrowth in MDD increased from 1145 (1993–2003)
to 5606 ha/year (2003–2013) and expanded mostly in abandoned agricultural areas as reported in previous studies
(Alvarez and Naughton-Treves 2003; Chavez 2014) and other
local, regional, and continental studies across tropical regions
(Sánchez-Cuervo and Aide 2013; Sánchez-Cuervo et al. 2012;
Teixeira et al. 2009).

Drivers of change
A major contributor to deforestation was gold mining. We
estimated the extent of gold mining increased by almost ten
times (938%) between 1993 and 2013, which is higher than
previous estimates from local studies in MDD: 239% between
2001 and 2011 (Scullion et al. 2014) and 400% between 1999
and 2012 (Asner et al. 2013). Certainly, there are differences
in regional gold mining total extent in the literature: Swenson
et al. (2011) found 15,500 ha of gold mining in 2009, Scullion
et al. (2014) found 32,642 ha in 2011, Asner et al. (2013)
found 47,000 ha in 2012, and we estimated 37,535 ha in
2013. These contrasting results are likely due to a mix of
factors, including differences in methodologies, time frames,
and study area sizes (Asner et al. 2013).
Overall, between 1993 and 2013, forest cover was mostly
converted to agriculture (470%) and mining areas (938%),
while urban areas (187%) expanded into surrounding

8

Page 10 of 14

agricultural lands. Deforestation in the period between 1993
and 2003 could be the result of President Alan Garcia’s
(1985–1990) economic and colonization policies (Alvarez
and Naughton-Treves 2003; Escobal D’Angelo 1992). This
government launched rural credit programs that fostered immigration from the Andes, resulting in a progressive increase
in regional crops such as rice and corn, the expansion of cattle
ranching, and urban growth of Puerto Maldonado and other
smaller towns in the north of MDD (Campbell et al. 1998;
Dourojeanni 2006). The northern towns had better opportunities for economic development and population growth than
the southern ones because of the improvement of the
Interoceanic Highway as well as their strategic location bordering Brazil. Population density in MDD increased because
of immigration rather than high fertility rates, which is a welldocumented driver of tropical deforestation (Geist and
Lambin 2002). The Interoceanic Highway and the immigration prompted an expansion of small-scale mining operations
along the Madre de Dios River and near wetlands (i.e.,
aguajales) and accelerated the expansion of existing largescale mining along the Colorado River (Asner et al. 2013).
In the second period (2003–2013), while the Interoceanic
Highway was being paved, deforestation increased mostly
along roads and around previously deforested areas where
agriculture, mining, and urban areas continued to expand.
The high deforestation rate during this period could be the
result of the economic policies of Presidents Alejandro
Toledo (2001–2006) and Alan Garcia (2006–2011), which
promoted a national economic growth agenda, neoliberal austerity, decentralization, agricultural mechanization, and infrastructure improvements (Baca et al. 2014; Chavez 2014). The
rise of international gold prices, increased accessibility of
heavy machinery, and sustained mercury imports could explain the sudden development of large-scale informal mining
operations (e.g., Guacamayo and Delta-1 areas) along the
Colorado, Inambari, and Malinowski rivers (Asner et al.
2013; Swenson et al. 2011) as well as other associated land
conversions to agriculture and urban areas (Asner et al. 2013).
The concomitant regression of agricultural lands in the southern portion of MDD observed in our results could indicate that
gold mining represented a more profitable alternative activity
for the people of Madre de Dios, as shown in other studies
(Scullion et al. 2014).
Like deforestation, forest regrowth also seems to be the
result of agricultural land abandonment potentially driven by
the policies of President Alberto Fujimori (1990–2000). The
Fujimori government promoted economic neoliberalism, fiscal conservatism, and austerity measures that kept infrastructure investments to a minimum and removed agricultural subsidies established by his predecessor (Baca et al. 2014),
resulting in agriculture abandonment with subsequent forest
regrowth (Alvarez and Naughton-Treves 2003; Chavez 2014).
The extensive forest regrowth observed in the period between

2003 and 2013 may be due to the MDD special project sustainable policies. Some of these policies included agricultural
diversification through agroforestry with the establishment of
tree plantations (e.g., rubber, copoazu, teak, mahogany;
Chavez et al. 2014). In some areas, these plantations could
have been classified as forest cover increasing its extent as
shown in other studies (Chavez et al. 2014).
In sum, the road network spreading, mining and agriculture
expansion, and population growth facilitated by national and
regional public policies and institutional changes could have
enabled the following landscape dynamics in MDD: (1) deforestation following the upgrade of the Interoceanic Highway
and expansion of secondary roads connected with the highway (Delgado 2008; Dourojeanni 2006); (2) agricultural expansion (at the expense of forest cover) along the highway and
secondary roads (Chavez et al. 2013); (3) illegal mining expansion (at the expense of forest and agricultural lands) along
secondary roads, rivers, and watercourses (Asner et al. 2013;
Swenson et al. 2011); and (4) land abandonment in rural areas
with subsequent forest regrowth (Alvarez and NaughtonTreves 2003; Chavez 2014). These dynamics affected MDD
landscape structure and promoted greater fragmentation in
some areas with direct consequences for biodiversity.

Landscape fragmentation
Decreasing LPI and MPS and increasing NP metrics show that
MDD landscape fragmentation increased from 1993 to 2013
(Chavez 2014) as in other areas across the Amazon basin
(Michalski et al. 2008). Regional fragmentation patterns varied in space and time: Area 3 (North) showed a large increase
of NP with a decrease in MPS as a result of agricultural activities (Chavez 2014), whereas Area 4 (South) showed the largest decrease in FC and LPI as a result of gold mining activities
in Huepetuhe, Delta-1, and Guacamayo. These two processes
were more intense for the period from 2003 to 2013 after the
Interoceanic Highway upgrade and the unpaved roads expansion. Road construction and land use dynamics have carved a
heterogeneous landscape, with the emergence of a large
number of smaller forest fragments embedded into an agriculture matrix to the north and a large gold mining footprint to the south. The influence of paved and unpaved
road proximity promoting deforestation and forest fragmentation is a recognized process in MDD (Alvarez and
Naughton-Treves 2003; Chavez et al. 2013; Delgado
2008), in the broader Amazon basin (Pfaff et al. 2007),
and in other tropical areas (Laurance et al. 2009).
Although the Interoceanic Highway is an important driver
of deforestation, the unpaved road network radiating from the
highway had a higher probability of deforestation in our results. Our results suggest a nonlinear process where deforestation was mainly driven by the proximity to the highway in
the 1993–2003 period, shifting toward the proximity to

unpaved roads as the main deforestation driver during the
2003–2013 period. A possible reason for the shift could be
the soil impoverishment and reduction in forest resources
closer to the highway that forced colonists to move into the
forest and build unpaved roads (Campbell et al. 1998). Similar
dynamics have been observed along highways in Bolivia
(Pando) and Brazil (Acre; Delgado 2008). The effect of the
unpaved roads network on the landscape is of great concern as
it penetrates deep into the forest to primary habitats and PAs
(Delgado 2008), becoming a serious threat to the connectivity
of the VACC.

Implications for biodiversity conservation
Our findings showed that MDD maintained 95% of its forest
cover by 2013 coupled with a scattered forest recovery that
certainly helped preserve biodiversity and ecosystem services
such as carbon sequestration and water yield. Unfortunately,
the quality of forest regrowth is highly influenced by the land
use history (e.g., previous agriculture or gold mining) and the
intensity of the disturbance (Chazdon 2003). A study by
Laurance (1999) found that a slashed and burned
agricultural area in the tropics might take between 100 and
200 years to recover its biomass and host species composition
similar to that of a mature forest. Novoa et al. (2016) found
that small abandoned mining areas transition to regenerating
forest with the establishment of fewer pioneer species than
other recovering areas. The soil degradation, high toxicity
levels (e.g., mercury), and large distance from seed sources
for recolonization in MDD largest gold mining areas (e.g.,
Huepetuhe, Delta-1, and Guacamayo) in Area 4 (South) make
full secondary succession even more challenging (RománDañobeytia et al. 2015) and affect the long-term connectivity
of the VACC.
The VACC represents a critical area to preserve the extraordinary biodiversity of the Tropical Andes and Amazonia
(CEPF 2005). Its northern portion in MDD holds the greatest
mammal, amphibian, and bird diversity when compared with
the rest of the corridor (Supplementary Material Fig. S1a).
Different types of land protection and closer monitoring of
areas outside and inside PAs are critical to avoid deforestation
in the VACC. Some studies have shown that large PAs (e.g.,
Tambopata National Reserve; Oliveira et al. 2007), private
managed conservation, and ecotourism concessions
(Vuohelainen et al. 2012) are all effective to protect against
deforestation. Nevertheless, land conversion persists in MDD
today, both outside and inside PAs, affecting landscape structure and the integrity and connectivity of the VACC. Inside
PAs and their buffer zones, gold mining and road networks
spread without territorial planning. Recent reports show that
gold mining expands inside the Tambopata and Amarakaeri
Reserves where 450 and 11 ha were deforested in 2016, respectively (Supplementary Material Fig. S1d; Finer and

Page 11 of 14

8

Novoa 2015; Finer et al. 2016a). In parallel, the unpaved road
network is expanding in the buffer zone between Amarakaeri
Communal Reserve and Manu National Park where 33 km of
roads deforested 32 ha in 2016 (Finer et al. 2016b). Landscape
changes are likely to continue because several primary roads
are projected to be built in MDD (e.g., Carretera Longitudinal
de la Selva southern sector – PE-5S). This issue is particularly
worrisome in Area 4 (South) where a high probability of
change from forest to mining and to agriculture is expected
(Supplementary Material Fig. S1b, c) affecting species viability and dispersal (Rutledge 2003) inside the VACC. Thus,
Area 4 (South) should be a priority target for on-the-ground
conservation actions including a land planning approach
(Alvarez and Naughton-Treves 2003; Asner et al. 2013;
Scullion et al. 2014). Forest fragmentation into smaller
patches outside PAs in Area 3 (North) due to small-scale
agriculture will likely promote isolation of remaining forests, animal population decline, and ultimately changes in
biology, behavior, and species interactions (Fischer and
Lindenmayer 2007; Rutledge 2003). At the same time,
forest loss due to the large gold mining footprint in Area
4 (South) may impede the survival of many species that
rely on the structure and composition of primary forest
(Alvarez-Berríos et al. 2016; Parry et al. 2007).

Conclusions
This research revealed that thousands of hectares of tropical
forest were lost between 1993 and 2013 in MDD due to unplanned development including the road network spreading,
mining and agriculture expansion, and population growth.
Although most of MDD remains preserved, important forest
areas have been cleared or fragmented, posing high risk of
biodiversity loss. The dramatic gold mining footprint in the
south is threatening species survival, the integrity of PAs, and
the connectivity of the VACC. Despite its enormous conservation value, the VACC lacks formal legal conservation status
and sustained management, probably because of the weak
binational coordination and the overlapping land use concessions across the corridor (REDPARQUES 2016). To preserve
the integrity and connectivity in MDD and thus in the VACC,
we recommend to the Peruvian national and regional governments to (1) revise the legal framework for the VACC and
encourage binational cooperation with Bolivia; (2) develop,
validate, and implement a sustainable management plan for
MDD; (3) enforce sustainable development projects such as
agroforestry along the Interoceanic Highway, helping colonists to use their land longer while maintaining ecosystem
functions and greater biodiversity and biomass (Campbell
et al. 1998; Sistla et al. 2016); (4) develop and implement
strategies to maintain the VACC connectivity by identifying
and preserving smaller animal movement corridors and

8

Page 12 of 14

establish restoration and reforestation programs in abandoned
mining areas in Area 4 (South); and (5) promote LULC changes and scenario planning research to understand development
impacts on biodiversity and ecosystem services before land
modification occurs. We hope the results and recommendations provided will encourage decision makers and the civil
society to engage MDD in a sustainable development path.
